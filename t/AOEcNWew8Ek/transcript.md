## Summary

Becky Jennings addresses a common parenting dilemma: whether rewarding ADHD kids for basic tasks counts as bribery or undermines internal motivation. She explains the neuroscience behind ADHD, noting that these kids' dopamine reward centers are quieter and less responsive to delayed gratification, so a reward promised too far in the future loses its motivating power through what researchers call steep discounting.

She describes rewards as jumper cables for a car with a weak starter: they are needed to get the engine (motivation) running, not as permanent payment for behavior. Small, immediate, specific rewards like praise, fake money, or stickers are far more effective than big delayed prizes, and they should be faded out over time as the child builds the underlying skill through repeated practice and simulation.

Jennings distinguishes true rewards from bribery. Bribery is giving in during a meltdown just to stop bad behavior in the moment, whereas a proper reward system is planned and agreed upon in advance, during a calm period, so the child knows exactly what behavior earns what reward. She references Alan Kazdin's research from the Yale Parenting Center as evidence supporting this micro-reward, practice-and-praise approach for aggressive, oppositional, and defiant behaviors often seen alongside ADHD.

She stresses that rewards should never be taken away as punishment, and that children should help co-create their own reward systems to increase buy-in. ### Key takeaways

- ADHD brains have a quieter reward center and need immediate dopamine triggers rather than relying on the anticipation of a future reward.
- About 78% of kids with combined type ADHD show steep discounting, meaning rewards lose value the longer they have to wait for them.
- Boring, non-preferred tasks don't light up the ADHD reward center, so kids may unconsciously create conflict to generate the stimulation needed to act.
- Small, immediate, frequent, and specific rewards like verbal praise or fake money are more effective than big delayed prizes.
- True rewards are planned in advance and agreed upon ahead of time, unlike bribery which is given reactively during a meltdown to stop bad behavior.
- Rewards should be gradually faded out as the child builds skill, acting as a temporary jumpstart rather than a permanent transaction.
- Earned rewards should never be taken away as punishment, since this undermines the child's trust that good behavior is reliably recognized.
- Involving the child in co-creating their own reward system increases their buy-in and investment in the behavior plan.
- Specific praise naming the exact positive behavior is more effective than vague praise like 'good job,' which can cause confusion or anxiety.
