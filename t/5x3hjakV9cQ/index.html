Summary

Becky Jennings recounts a bedtime conflict with her ADHD son that turned into a surprisingly candid conversation about what he experiences when asked to do non-preferred tasks. He describes feeling controlled by "energy," experiencing refusal requests as literal electric shocks, and having his thoughts go "pitch black" when overwhelmed. Jennings connects each of his descriptions to specific studies on dopamine, delay aversion, amygdala reactivity, working memory, and inner speech development in ADHD children.

The core insight is that non-preferred tasks are processed by the ADHD brain as a physical threat, which fires the amygdala, knocks the prefrontal cortex offline, and shuts down the inner voice that would normally help a child self-correct in the moment. This explains why logic and reasoning don't work during a meltdown, and why yelling sometimes becomes the only "spark" intense enough to get a frozen child moving.

Her son also revealed that he never misbehaves at school because he gets frequent built-in rewards (recess, lunch, positive teacher feedback), while home interactions skew more corrective. Jennings uses this to argue parents need to build in choice, quick wins, breaks, and praise rather than relying on anger to motivate action, and she ties this into promotion for her coaching program's masterclass and a January two-day planning event.

Takeaways

  • ADHD kids often experience requests to do non-preferred tasks as a physical threat, not just an annoyance.
  • Dopamine in ADHD brains tends to get stuck on the reward itself rather than linking it to the task needed to earn it, making delayed rewards feel nearly worthless.
  • Waiting and boredom activate the amygdala and insula in ADHD brains, producing a real felt sense of discomfort rather than mere impatience.
  • When the amygdala fires, the prefrontal cortex goes offline, taking logic, working memory, and communication with it, which is why reasoning doesn't work mid-meltdown.
  • The self-guided inner voice that helps most people adjust their behavior develops later in ADHD kids, so external narration from parents can help fill that gap.
  • Kids with ADHD often know what they want to do and even want to cooperate, but struggle with follow-through due to these brain-based barriers.
  • Yelling can inadvertently work as an arousal spike that activates an under-aroused ADHD brain, which is why kids may wait for it before acting.
  • Immediate, frequent small rewards and praise (like what often happens at school) are more effective motivators for ADHD kids than delayed consequences.
  • Building practice, play, novelty, and built-in breaks into routines can replace yelling as the spark needed to start tasks.
